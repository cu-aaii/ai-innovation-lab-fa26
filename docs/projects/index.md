# How Projects Work

Every team in the AI Innovation Lab works on a **real project for a real Cornell stakeholder.** This page explains how that works — from how projects get selected to how your semester is structured around them.

---

## The Project Pipeline

Each semester, Cornell units submit problems they need solved. The AI Hub team reviews submissions for feasibility, data risk, scope, and student fit. Selected projects are matched with student teams.

```
Cornell unit submits a problem
        ↓
AI Hub reviews and scopes
        ↓
Project selected, team assigned
        ↓
Tech Lead onboarded, tools provisioned
        ↓
12-week sprint cycle
        ↓
Demo Day + Client Handoff
```

You don't choose your project — you're assigned. If you have concerns about your assignment, talk to your TA in Week 1.

---

## Sprint Structure

Each sprint is **one week long.** Your sprint rhythm looks like this:

| Day | Activity |
|---|---|
| **Monday** | All-hands (2:00–4:30 PM, CIS 250) — clinic, progress sharing, instructor time |
| **TBD** | Stakeholder sprint meeting (30 min) — demo, feedback, requirements |
| **TBD** | Tech Lead check-in (30 min) — blockers, architecture, code review |
| **Friday 5 PM** | A1 reflection + A2 4-Box due |

---

## Your Role on the Team

**Students** own the build. You are not assistants — you are engineers responsible for delivering a working product to a real client.

**What you own:**
- The code in your team's GitHub repo
- The quality of your weekly status reports
- Your own contributions, as measured by commit history and stakeholder meeting engagement
- The final product and handoff package

**What Tech Leads handle:**
- Technical mentorship and architecture direction
- Sprint sign-offs and code reviews
- Provisioning tool access and credentials
- Assessing your technical contribution (A4)

**What TAs handle:**
- Grades, Canvas, and assignment questions
- Attendance tracking
- Conflict mediation

---

## What Makes a Good Sprint

At the end of every sprint, you should be able to point to something **new that works.** Not "we made progress" — something a stakeholder can see, click, or test.

Working in small, deployable increments is the skill. The teams that struggle at Demo Day are the ones that tried to build everything at once in the last two weeks.
