## Overview

Your final presentation tells the story of what you built, why it matters, and what you learned. The audience includes your classmates, instructor, TA, Tech Leads, and when possible your client.

Think of it as: **product pitch + technical demo + honest retrospective.**

- **Length:** 7–10 minutes, evenly distributed among team members
- **Format:** Live in-class presentation on Dec 7; upload video + slides to Canvas

---

## Required Structure

| # | Section | Time | What to cover |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Problem & context | ~2 min | What problem does your client have? Why does it need an AI solution? |
| 2 | What you built | ~2 min | What is the tool? What does it do? What AI techniques does it use (RAG, agents, fine-tuning, etc.)? |
| 3 | Live or recorded demo | ~2 min | Show the tool working on a realistic use case. Show at least one edge case or limitation. |
| 4 | Technical decisions | ~2 min | What were the 2–3 most important technical choices you made? What did you consider and why? |
| 5 | What you learned | ~2 min | What surprised you? What would you do differently? What are you most proud of? |

---

## Slide Guidelines

- Aim for **7–10 slides** total
- Minimize text on slides — you are the presentation, not your slides
- Include at least **one architecture diagram**
- Include **real screenshots or GIFs** of your tool

---

## Grading Rubric

This is worth 10 points. The score reflects narrative, demo, technical communication, and team dynamics together.

=== "10 — Exemplary"
    Clear narrative arc from problem → user → solution → impact. Every section connects to the next. Demo runs smoothly on a realistic input and a deliberate edge case is shown and explained. Technical decisions are framed as tradeoffs (not just choices). Team is honest about limitations and what they would do with another month. Time-managed within range. All members speak.

=== "9 — Strong"
    Strong narrative with one or two small gaps in flow. Demo runs cleanly on the main use case; the edge case is mentioned but not shown. Technical decisions are explained well. Team is honest about limitations. All members speak. Time-managed within range.

=== "8 — Solid"
    Audience can follow the narrative. Demo works but is somewhat scripted — runs the same query that obviously succeeds. Technical decisions are stated but not explored. Limitations are acknowledged in passing. Most team members speak; one or two are clearly more comfortable. Slightly over or under time.

=== "7 — Adequate"
    Narrative is present but uneven — audience may be unsure why the problem matters or how the tool is used. Demo is shared but not well narrated. Technical decisions are listed without justification. Limitations not really acknowledged. Speaking is unevenly distributed; one or two members carry most of it. Significantly over or under time.

=== "6 — Weak"
    Audience is unclear on what was built or why it matters. No functioning demo. Technical content is generic ("we used LangChain"). No discussion of tradeoffs or limitations. One person dominates the talk. Significantly over or under time.

=== "0 — Not submitted"
    Video and/or slides not submitted. Or team did not present on Demo Day.

---

!!! tip "What makes a demo land"
    Show a **realistic input** — not a toy example, but something your actual client would type or submit. Then deliberately show an edge case or failure mode, and explain what's happening. This demonstrates you understand the tool, not just that it can demo well. Hiding limitations does the opposite.
